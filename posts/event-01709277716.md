# A Cosmic Puzzle Solved in Real Time: EP260806a

On August 6, 2026, Starithm's monitoring systems caught something extraordinary unfolding across the electromagnetic spectrum. The Einstein Probe space telescope had detected a bright X-ray transient, and within hours, ground-based observatories worldwide pivoted to follow it. What emerged was a textbook example of modern multi-wavelength astronomy—and Starithm tracked every development live as the community worked to decode this mysterious event.

## Alert Timeline

The story began at **23:02:28 UTC on August 6, 2026**, when Einstein Probe's Wide-field X-ray Telescope (WXT) triggered on a new X-ray source at **RA = 12.28°, Dec = 35.59°**. The detection carried a signal-to-noise ratio of **SNR = 7** with an impressive **0.5–4 keV flux of ~1.65 × 10⁻¹⁰ erg/s/cm²**—bright enough to demand immediate attention. Within minutes, Einstein Probe's Follow-up X-ray Telescope (FXT) confirmed the detection, locking in the coordinates and triggering a cascade of follow-up observations.

The initial X-ray data suggested something extraordinary: *a transient with a rapidly declining flux profile*, hinting at either a compact object merger or an outburst from a supermassive black hole. The **estimated peak luminosity of 3.8 × 10⁴⁵ erg/s** in the 0.5–10 keV band placed this firmly in the high-energy event category—comparable to some of the most violent phenomena in the universe.

## What the Community Found

Within hours, optical telescopes converged on the coordinates. The Teide Observatory's LCO 1m telescope reported an optical counterpart at **magnitude 19.32 in the SDSS-r filter**, while the Gran Telescopio Canarias spectroscopy revealed the smoking gun: strong emission lines at a common redshift of **z = 0.089**—placing the source roughly 1.2 billion light-years away.

The optical data painted a consistent picture. The Liverpool Telescope detected a **blue color** consistent with an active galactic nucleus outburst, while the SVOM/VT telescope caught the source brightening by approximately **0.1 magnitude over just 3.1 hours**—evidence of rapid variability. By August 11, the Maidanak Observatory measured the source at **magnitude 18.16 in the R-band**, showing continued evolution.

But the real surprise came from the radio observations. Multiple facilities detected a brightened radio source with **flux densities of ~5.5 mJy at 10 GHz and ~50 mJy at 104.5 GHz**—and critically, this spectrum was flat to inverted, *suggesting a relativistic jet similar to those seen in gamma-ray bursts or tidal disruption events*.
