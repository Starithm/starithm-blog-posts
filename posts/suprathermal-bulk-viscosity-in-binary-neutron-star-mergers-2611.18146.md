## Why This Matters

When two neutron stars collide, the violent merger creates extreme conditions that push matter far beyond its normal equilibrium state. A new study by Tambe and Chatterjee reveals that standard models for how this matter dissipates energy have been incomplete—and that the actual energy loss could be dramatically larger than previously calculated. This finding has direct implications for how we interpret gravitational wave signals and electromagnetic observations from these cataclysmic events.

## What They Found

The researchers focused on bulk viscosity, a property that describes how matter resists density changes. In neutron star mergers and core-collapse supernovae, large-amplitude density oscillations in the kHz range drive the system far from chemical equilibrium. The Urca process—a weak interaction that converts protons to neutrons and vice versa—acts as a brake, damping these oscillations through bulk viscous forces.

Previous calculations of bulk viscosity assumed small-amplitude oscillations, where the deviation from chemical equilibrium (μ_Δ) remains smaller than the temperature T. Tambe and Chatterjee computed the complete amplitude-dependent bulk viscosity for the first time, incorporating two previously neglected effects: thermal blurring of particle states and collisional broadening of nucleons in the dense medium.

Their results show that non-linear effects become significant at surprisingly modest amplitudes. When oscillation amplitudes reach approximately **10 percent of the background density**, the bulk viscosity can increase by **three to five orders of magnitude** compared to linear predictions. This is not a marginal correction—it represents a fundamental change in how the system dissipates energy.

## Why It Matters

The implications extend across multi-messenger astronomy. Gravitational wave signals from mergers encode information about the equation of state and transport properties of neutron star matter. If bulk viscosity is orders of magnitude larger than previously assumed in certain regimes, this changes how merger simulations evolve and what gravitational waveforms we should expect to detect. Similarly, the electromagnetic counterparts to mergers—kilonovae and other transients—depend sensitively on the dynamics of the merger remnant, which is governed by viscous damping of oscillations.

For researchers interpreting observations from detectors like LIGO and Virgo, this work suggests that existing numerical simulations may need revision to accurately capture the energy dissipation in merging neutron stars. The finding also applies to core-collapse supernovae, where similar density oscillations occur.

## What's Next

The authors note that their results have "important consequences" for simulations and observations, but the paper does not detail specific follow-up studies. The natural next steps would involve incorporating these corrected viscosity calculations into full numerical relativity simulations and comparing the resulting gravitational waveforms and merger dynamics against current models. Observational tests will require comparing predictions with future detections.
