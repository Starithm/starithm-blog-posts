# A Distant Explosion Across the Cosmos: How Starithm Caught GRB 261029A in Real Time

On the morning of October 29, 2026, the universe delivered a spectacular reminder of its violent extremes. A gamma-ray burst exploded into existence at **z = 3.27**—so distant that its light has traveled for over 11 billion years to reach us—and Starithm's monitoring systems caught every photon of its arrival. Within seconds of Fermi's trigger, our platform was aggregating alerts, tracking position refinements, and correlating detections across the electromagnetic spectrum. This is what real-time transient astronomy looks like: a coordinated global response to a cosmic explosion unfolding in near-live time.

## Alert Timeline: The First Minutes

The cascade began at **01:27:03 UT** when Fermi's Gamma-ray Burst Monitor (GBM) detected the initial spike of high-energy radiation. Within the same second, Fermi-LAT went offline—a standard precaution when pointing toward a bright transient—while SVOM's ECLAIRs instrument independently confirmed the burst just 4 seconds later at **01:27:07 UT**. 

What followed was a rapid dance of positional refinement. Fermi's flight software issued six successive localization notices over the next minute, the position gradually converging as more photons accumulated in the detector. The initial rough position near **RA = 357.7°, Dec = 47.4°** (in the northern sky) gave way to ground-based analysis, which narrowed the error circle to the final localization: **RA = 12.25°, Dec = 32.32°** with a **3.6-degree statistical uncertainty**. This refinement was crucial—it gave ground-based observers a manageable search region within minutes of the trigger.

## What the Community Found

By the time Fermi's final position notice settled, robotic telescopes were already slewing. GOTO spotted the optical counterpart—designated **AT 2026ahaw**—within the error region, initially at **L = 13.98 AB mag** and fading rapidly to **14.85 AB mag**. This was the smoking gun: a bright optical transient coincident with the gamma-ray burst.

Within 2–3 hours, a global network mobilized. The Thai Robotic Telescope measured **R = 17.41** at 2.3 hours post-trigger. KAIT at Lick Observatory confirmed **R = 17.15** shortly after. Infrared observations from MDM/MIRAGE revealed a near-infrared counterpart at **J = 16.32** and **Hs = 16.45**, showing *a bright, multi-wavelength signature consistent with a classical long GRB afterglow*. The Nordic Telescope then delivered the breakthrough: spectroscopic confirmation at **z = 3.27**, pinpointing this explosion to the early universe.

High-energy observations sealed the picture. Fermi-LAT detected significant photon flux extending to **2.5 GeV**—an exceptionally high energy for a distant burst, *suggesting a powerful relativistic jet and efficient particle acceleration*. The burst exhibited a **T90 duration of ~40–47 seconds**, characteristic of a *long GRB likely originating from massive star core collapse*.
