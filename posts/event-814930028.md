# A Distant Explosion Across the Cosmos: How Starithm Caught GRB 261029A in Real Time

On the morning of October 29, 2026, the universe delivered a spectacular reminder of its violent extremes. A gamma-ray burst exploded into existence at **z = 3.27**—so distant that its light has traveled for over 11 billion years to reach us—and Starithm followed it from the first alert. Within seconds of Fermi's trigger, our platform was aggregating alerts, tracking position refinements, and correlating detections across the electromagnetic spectrum. This is what real-time transient astronomy looks like: a coordinated global response to a cosmic explosion unfolding in near-live time.

## Alert Timeline: The First Minutes

The cascade began at **01:27:03 UT** when Fermi's Gamma-ray Burst Monitor (GBM) detected the initial spike of high-energy radiation. SVOM's ECLAIRs instrument triggered on the same burst independently at **01:27:07 UT**, and Fermi's Large Area Telescope (LAT) also detected it (GCN 46085). 

What followed was a rapid dance of positional refinement. Fermi's flight software issued five successive localization notices over the next minute, the position gradually converging as more photons accumulated in the detector. The initial rough position near **RA = 357.75°, Dec = +46.15°** (in the northern sky) gave way to ground-based analysis, which narrowed the error circle to the final localization: **RA = 12.25°, Dec = 32.32°** with a **3.6-degree statistical uncertainty**. This refinement was crucial—it gave ground-based observers a manageable search region within minutes of the trigger.

![Fermi GBM light curve showing the complex multi-peaked structure of GRB 261029A](https://heasarc.gsfc.nasa.gov/FTP/fermi/data/gbm/triggers/2026/bn261029060/quicklook/glg_lc_medres34_bn261029060.gif)

## What the Community Found

By the time Fermi's final position notice settled, robotic telescopes were already slewing. GOTO spotted the optical counterpart—designated **AT 2026ahaw**—within the error region, initially at **L = 13.98 AB mag** and fading rapidly to **14.85 AB mag**. This was the smoking gun: a bright optical transient coincident with the gamma-ray burst.

Within 2–3 hours, a global network mobilized. The Thai Robotic Telescope measured **R = 17.41** at 2.3 hours post-trigger. KAIT at Lick Observatory confirmed **R = 17.15** shortly after. Infrared observations from MDM/MIRAGE revealed a near-infrared counterpart at **J = 16.32** and **Hs = 16.45**, showing *a bright, multi-wavelength signature consistent with a classical long GRB afterglow*. The Nordic Optical Telescope (NOT) then delivered the breakthrough: a spectroscopic redshift of **z = 3.27** (GCN 46081), placing this explosion in the early universe.

High-energy observations completed the picture. Fermi-LAT detected emission above 100 MeV, with its highest-energy photon at **2.5 GeV**, 44 seconds after the trigger (GCN 46085). In GBM the burst lasted **about 43 seconds (T90)** over two emission episodes (GCN 46098), characteristic of a *long GRB, the kind associated with the collapse of a massive star*.
